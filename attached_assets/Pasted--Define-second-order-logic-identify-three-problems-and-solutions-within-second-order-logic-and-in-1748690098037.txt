
Define second-order logic, identify three problems and solutions within second-order logic, and include proofs. Discuss why second-order logic is inadequate for grounding logicism, providing formal proofs and a precise definition of logicism, employing appropriate notation throughout.

**Second-Order Logic Defined**: Second-order logic extends first-order logic by allowing quantification not only over individual variables but also over relation and function variables. This increased expressive strength permits direct formulation of propositions about properties and relations.

**Problem 1**: Consider the challenge of expressing the notion of "finiteness" in second-order logic. Traditional first-order logic cannot adequately express such properties due to its limitations in quantification.

**Solution and Proof 1**: In second-order logic, we can express finiteness by stating: for a set \( S \), there exists a natural number \( n \) that serves as the cardinality of \( S \). This is formalized as:
\[
\exists n (\forall X ((X(0) \land \forall y (X(y) \rightarrow X(y+1)) \land \forall z (X(z) \rightarrow z < n)) \rightarrow \forall z (S(z) \rightarrow X(z))))
\]
This statement ensures that a set is finite iff it can be mapped bijectively to some initial segment of the natural numbers.

**Problem 2**: Consider the difficulty in expressing continuity of a function in first-order logic.

**Solution and Proof 2**: In second-order logic, continuity can be articulated by quantifying over all properties of subsets. For a function \( f \), it is continuous if, for any property \( P \), the preimage of \( P \) under \( f \) is open. Formally:
\[
\forall P (\forall x (P(x) \rightarrow \exists \delta (\forall y (|x-y| < \delta \rightarrow P(y)))) \rightarrow \forall x (\exists \delta (\forall y (|x-y| < \delta \rightarrow P(f(y))))))
\]
This captures the essence of continuity by ensuring that preimages of open sets under \( f \) remain open.

**Problem 3**: Address the task of expressing the concept of "well-ordering" within the framework of second-order logic.

**Solution and Proof 3**: Well-ordering can be succinctly stated in second-order logic by asserting that every non-empty subset has a least element. This is expressed as:
\[
\forall S (\emptyset \neq S \rightarrow \exists m (m \in S \land \forall n (n \in S \rightarrow m \leq n)))
\]
This statement embodies the principle that any subset has a minimal element, characterizing a well-ordered set.
